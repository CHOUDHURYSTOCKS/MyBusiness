One simple idea

A bond is a debt instrument: the investor lends money to an issuer for defined terms, generally receiving interest and principal repayment according to the instrument's terms.

Investor→ lends →Issuer→ pays →Interest + Principal

1. Bond Market in India

The Indian bond market connects borrowers that need long-term funding with investors seeking fixed-income exposure.

🏛️ Government securities

Central Government securities include Treasury Bills for short maturities and dated G-Secs for longer maturities. State Governments issue State Development Loans (SDLs).

RBI describes G-Secs as tradeable instruments acknowledging government debt.

🏢 Corporate debt

Companies and financial institutions can raise debt through bonds, debentures and other debt securities. Credit quality, security, covenants, maturity and liquidity matter.

Higher perceived credit risk generally requires higher expected yield.

🔄 Primary + secondary market

Primary: securities are issued to raise funds. Secondary: existing securities are traded. A bond's market price can move even when its coupon is fixed.

CouponContractual interest rate/payment

How a bond transaction works

01 • IssuanceIssuer sets terms and raises funds.
02 • AllocationInvestors receive securities.
03 • HoldingCoupon/other contractual cash flows occur.
04 • TradingInvestor may sell before maturity at market price, subject to liquidity.
05 • MaturityIssuer repays principal according to the terms.

Why bond markets matter

  • Finance government expenditure and infrastructure.
  • Provide companies with an alternative to bank borrowing/equity.
  • Give investors a range of fixed-income risk/return choices.
  • Provide market signals about interest rates and credit risk.

Advanced lens: the yield curve

The yield curve plots yields against maturities for comparable debt. Its shape can reflect expectations, term premium, liquidity and supply-demand conditions.

Do not interpret curve shape as a standalone buy/sell signal.

2. Indian Bond Terminology — Interactive Glossary

Search terms such as coupon, YTM, accrued interest, G-Sec, SDL, NCD, duration, credit spread and more.

Face / Par Value
The nominal amount used for contractual calculations such as coupon and redemption.
Coupon Rate
The stated interest rate on face value. Coupon and market yield are not the same thing.
Yield
A return measure that relates bond cash flows to its current price; the exact meaning depends on the yield measure.
YTM
Yield to Maturity: an annualized return measure assuming stated cash flows and holding to maturity, subject to the calculation's assumptions.
Current Yield
Annual coupon divided by the market price; it ignores some price effects and time value.
Accrued Interest
Interest accumulated since the last coupon date, relevant to clean/dirty price conventions.
Clean Price
Quoted bond price excluding accrued interest in markets using clean-price convention.
Dirty Price
Clean price plus accrued interest, representing the settlement value under that convention.
Maturity
Date on which principal is contractually due, subject to the security's terms and issuer performance.
Duration
A measure related to the sensitivity of a bond's price to changes in yield; modified duration is commonly used as a price-sensitivity approximation.
Credit Rating
An external opinion about credit risk. It is useful but not a guarantee of repayment.
Credit Spread
Yield difference between a credit-risky bond and a reference/benchmark of similar characteristics.
G-Sec
Government Security issued by the Central or State Government; RBI identifies T-Bills and dated securities as key government-security categories.
SDL
State Development Loan: State Government dated securities.
T-Bill
Short-term Government of India security with original maturity below one year.
NCD
Non-Convertible Debenture: a corporate debt instrument that does not convert into equity under its terms.
Secured Debt
Debt supported by specified security/charge, subject to the legal documentation and enforcement framework.
Unsecured Debt
Debt without specified collateral security; recovery can be more exposed to issuer credit risk.
Callable Bond
A bond that the issuer may redeem before scheduled maturity under specified terms.
Puttable Bond
A bond giving the investor a contractual right to sell/redeem early under specified terms.
Zero-Coupon Bond
No periodic coupon; issued/purchased below redemption value in structures where the return comes from the price difference.
Primary Market
Market where new securities are issued.
Secondary Market
Market where already-issued securities are bought and sold.
Liquidity Risk
Risk that an investor cannot sell promptly at a fair market price.
Interest-Rate Risk
Risk that market-rate changes cause the bond's price to move against the investor.
Default Risk
Risk that the issuer fails to meet contractual interest or principal obligations.
Reinvestment Risk
Risk that future coupon cash flows cannot be reinvested at the expected rate.
Tax / Post-Tax Return
Return after applicable taxes; compare investments on a consistent post-tax basis.

Bond Price ↔ Interest Rate Relationship

For an existing fixed-coupon bond, market price generally moves inversely to market yields, all else equal.

Yield ↓ → Price ↑Yield ↑ → Price ↓

Simple numerical intuition

Suppose a bond has face value ₹1,000 and a fixed 8% annual coupon, so it pays ₹80 per year.

  • If comparable market yields fall below 8%, that ₹80 cash flow becomes relatively attractive → price can rise above ₹1,000.
  • If comparable market yields rise above 8%, the ₹80 cash flow becomes relatively less attractive → price can fall below ₹1,000.
The actual price depends on all contractual cash flows, time to maturity, yield, credit quality, liquidity and other market factors.

3. Bonds vs. Debentures

In common Indian usage the terms overlap, but the legal form, issuer, security, convertibility and documentation can differ.

FeatureBondDebenture
Core ideaDebt instrument evidencing borrowing.Corporate debt instrument; in Indian educational usage, often associated with company-issued debt.
Typical issuerGovernment, public-sector entity, financial institution or company depending on instrument.Typically a corporate issuer.
SecurityMay be secured or unsecured depending on issue terms.May be secured or unsecured; never assume “debenture” automatically means unsecured.
ConvertibilityCan be convertible or non-convertible depending on terms.May be NCD, PCD or FCD structures depending on issue terms.
Investor returnCoupon/other contractual payments + possible price gain/loss.Interest/other contractual payments + possible price gain/loss.
Main riskInterest-rate, credit, liquidity, reinvestment and other risks.Especially issuer credit, security/enforcement, liquidity and interest-rate risks.
Important: “Bond” and “debenture” are not perfectly interchangeable legal labels. Always read the offer document/issue terms, security details, covenants, ranking and redemption conditions.

4. Types of Bonds — With Indian Examples

Use the type to understand the cash-flow and risk structure—not as a guarantee of safety or return.

SOVEREIGN

Government Bonds / Dated G-Secs

Debt issued by the Government of India with defined coupon and maturity terms. Market prices can still fluctuate before maturity.

Example: A 10-year Government of India security paying a fixed coupon.
SHORT TERM

Treasury Bills

Short-term Government of India securities with original maturity below one year. They are commonly issued at a discount and redeemed at face value.

Example: A 91-day T-Bill bought below ₹100 and redeemed at ₹100.
STATE

State Development Loans

Dated securities issued by State Governments. They form part of India's government securities market.

Example: An SDL issued by a State Government with a stated coupon and maturity.
CORPORATE

Corporate Bonds

Debt issued by companies or financial institutions. Credit spread, rating, covenants and liquidity are central considerations.

Example: A listed company issues a 5-year bond at a fixed coupon.
NON-CONVERTIBLE

NCDs

Non-convertible debentures pay according to their contractual terms and do not convert into equity under the instrument's terms.

Example: A company issues a 3-year secured NCD with a stated coupon.
CONVERTIBLE

Convertible Bonds / Debentures

Debt that can convert into equity according to predefined conditions. Conversion can change the investor's risk and return profile.

Example: A corporate instrument allowing conversion into equity at a specified price/ratio.
ZERO COUPON

Zero-Coupon Bonds

No periodic coupon. The investor's return arises from the difference between purchase/issue price and redemption value, subject to terms.

Example: Buy for ₹900 and receive ₹1,000 at maturity, ignoring taxes and other costs.
INFLATION LINKED

Inflation-Linked Structures

Designed so some component of cash flows or principal references inflation. Structure-specific terms determine the actual protection.

Example: An inflation-linked government security where eligible cash flows are adjusted under its rules.
OPTIONALITY

Callable / Puttable Bonds

Callable instruments can be redeemed early by the issuer; puttable instruments can provide investors an early-exit right under specified conditions.

Example: A 7-year bond with an issuer call option after year 3.
TAX / PURPOSE

Tax-Advantaged / Special-Purpose Bonds

Some issues may have tax or eligibility features established by specific laws/rules. Never assume a bond is tax-free without checking the current issue terms and tax rules.

Example: A specifically notified tax-benefit bond, where applicable.
GOLD-LINKED

Sovereign Gold Bonds

A government security linked to the price of gold rather than a conventional fixed-coupon bond. Availability and tax treatment depend on current rules.

Example: An SGB unit representing a specified quantity of gold, with interest under its scheme terms.
STRUCTURED

Structured Debt

Debt whose payoff can depend on specified reference assets, rates or formulas. Complexity makes document review particularly important.

Example: A debt security whose redemption/coupon references a predefined market variable.

Risk Checklist

A high coupon is not automatically a high-quality investment. Evaluate the whole instrument.

Learning Summary

  • Bond = lending money to an issuer under defined terms.
  • Coupon is contractual; yield is market-return related.
  • Fixed-coupon bond prices generally move opposite to market yields.
  • Holding to maturity does not remove issuer/default risk.
  • Government securities can still have market-price volatility before maturity.
  • Corporate debt requires credit and liquidity analysis.
  • “High rating” is not a guarantee.
  • Compare investments using risk, liquidity, tax and post-tax return—not coupon alone.

Quick Self-Test

Click each question to reveal the answer.

Why can a bond with an 8% coupon trade below ₹1,000?

Because its market yield may have risen above the bond's coupon rate, making its fixed cash flows less attractive relative to newly available yields.

Does a high coupon mean low risk?

No. A high coupon may compensate investors for higher credit, liquidity, structural or other risks.

Is a government bond price always ₹1,000?

No. Face value and market price are different concepts. A bond can trade above or below face value before maturity.

What should I check before buying a corporate bond?

Issuer financial strength, rating and rating rationale, security/ranking, maturity, yield, liquidity, covenants, call/put features, tax treatment and the offer document.
